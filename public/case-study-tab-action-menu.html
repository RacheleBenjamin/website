Push toward simplicity

  • Design system change demands simplicity.
  • Vertical tabs as an existing simple solution.

What’s known

  • People want help managing tabs.
  • All their tools in one place appeals to tab-heavy users.

What’s unknown

  • Whether the simpler direction would still serve people.
  • Whether vertical tabs DSAT is a reason to avoid it.
  • Whether this concept has appeal beyond the most tab-heavy cohort.
Plan

Study setup to help the team decide

Method

Unmoderated testing of discoverability, impressions, and usefulness. Two user groups, 8 participants each: tab-heavy, tab-light. Mega-menu tested after Tab Center.

What this added

More confidence about how each direction behaved across different usage patterns, not just inside one narrow power-user cohort.

Why this stage mattered

  • The team still couldn’t rely on “which one they liked better.”
  • The richer question was whether flexibility actually solved the problem better, or just added complexity.
  • This stage set up the recommendation by checking whether people got stuck, whether the pattern converted people into vertical-tab users, and whether engineering cost was worth it.
What this section sets up

The next step wasn’t just another round of preference testing. It was a way to see whether a more flexible concept created enough real behavior change to justify building it.

Recommendation

Lower the cost, help users discover vertical tabs

My recommendation was to build the simpler one. The team didn’t have signal that people were getting stuck in it, and there was signal that it could convert people into a vertical-tabs-is-solving-the-problem user group.

Recommendation

Build the simpler one. Lower the engineering cost, help users discover vertical tabs, and use the lighter-weight direction to move more people into a problem-solving behavior instead of over-investing in flexibility too early.

No clear signal that people were stuck in the simpler pattern.
Some people got into vertical tabs and wanted to keep it forever.
The stronger upside looked like conversion into a vertical-tabs user group, not maximum surface flexibility.
What I was giving up
  • The more flexible concept had genuine appeal.
  • It combined the best parts of Mega Menu and Tab Centre.
  • Some users may have benefited from the added control.
Why I still recommended it
  • The feedback was still balanced / mixed.
  • I didn’t have a strong reason to spend more engineering cost yet.
  • The simpler pattern looked like enough to learn from in product.
Decision logic

The recommendation wasn’t “build the one people liked better.” It was: build the cheaper one, because the evidence did not justify more complexity, and the product could still learn a lot by helping users discover vertical tabs.
